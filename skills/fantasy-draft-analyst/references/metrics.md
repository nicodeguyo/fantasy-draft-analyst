# Player evaluation: measure opportunity, price and uncertainty

Use this reference to research a player, not as a substitute for sourced projections. No threshold below is a universal predictive law. Define the denominator, season, games and sample size; compare with the same-position population from the same dataset. Report unavailable metrics explicitly. Do not invent percentiles or convert a prose opinion into points.

## What strong looks like by position

| Position | Evidence of a strong fantasy role | Important checks |
|---|---|---|
| RB | Large carry share, receiving routes/targets, short-yardage and goal-line work | Competitor roles, expected games, offensive line, team scoring environment; snaps alone hide valuable touches |
| WR | Full-time routes, sustained target earning, useful air-yard and red-zone opportunity | Targets per route and yards per route with denominators, team passing volume, coverage and teammate availability |
| TE | Routes rather than blocking snaps, a meaningful share of team targets, red-zone involvement | TE premium, route participation, target competition; touchdowns alone can overstate repeatable value |
| QB | Designed rushing opportunity and efficient, sufficiently high passing volume | Scoring for passing TDs/INTs, sacks/pressure, protection, coaching, expected games; positional scarcity in 1QB versus superflex |
| K | Secure job, field-goal opportunities, range and favorable environment | League distance scoring, team fourth-down choices, weather; avoid spending early picks on narrow projected edges |
| DEF | Pressure generation and favorable opponent scoring/turnover context | Early schedule, opponent QB/line, home/away and market totals; defensive TDs are volatile |

“Elite” should mean a sourced percentile or positional rank in a defined sample, not a memorized cutoff. A 25% target share means different opportunity in offenses with 450 versus 650 attempts. A strong bench pick adds usable coverage or plausible role upside to this roster; another similar WR may add less than an RB with fewer projected points.

## Metrics and how to use them

Separate role (routes, carries, targets), efficiency (yards per route/carry, success rate), environment (pace, pass rate, offensive quality) and availability. Opportunity changes can dominate historical efficiency. Small samples need wider uncertainty, not a hard rule that every observation below a fixed count is worthless. Split usage with and without injured teammates, but show both sample sizes and avoid selecting only the most favorable stretch.

Age, draft capital and contracts are priors about role and development. They are not automatic cliffs or guarantees. Coach quotes require attribution and a date; verify whether subsequent practice, transactions or game usage supersede them. Injury designations do not map to an invented universal probability of playing.

## Betting markets

Record sportsbook/source, observed time, season/week, market, line, both prices if available and settlement rules. Win totals inform team environment; weekly spreads/totals inform scoring context. Player props can challenge a stat projection but do not cover all fantasy components. A prop line is not automatically an expected value, median or ceiling. Do not infer expected wins by subtracting a fixed amount for juice. Multiple books can share information and do not constitute independent projection models.

When accessible, compare a projection with several current markets after accounting for definitions and expected games. If data is unavailable, mark it unavailable; never imply betting-market validation occurred. No automatic betting-market blending is implemented in this release.

## A decision card

For each material choice show: actual pick and legal alternatives; league-scored source forecasts and their dates; matched ADP and format; two or three verified role metrics with denominators; injury/role events and supersession; roster coverage impact; take-now versus wait scenario; contrary evidence; and what would change the verdict. Separate facts, modeled estimates and analyst assumptions.

Avoid hindsight in draft reviews. Judge the choice using evidence available at the pick, then report subsequent outcomes separately. A good result does not prove the recommendation process was good, and an injury does not by itself prove it was bad.
